Everything works!

Some important functions we will use are:

  • smf.ols("y ~ x", data=df).fit() fits a linear regression model by OLS, and model.summary() prints the full regression output.
  • model.params, model.tvalues and model.pvalues give the coefficient estimates, $t$ values and $p$-values, and model.fvalue and model.f_pvalue give the $F$-statistic and its $p$-value.
  • model.conf_int(alpha=0.05) gives the $100(1-\alpha)\%$ confidence intervals for the coefficients.
  • model.predict(new_df) gives the point estimate of the mean response, and model.get_prediction(new_df).summary_frame(alpha=0.05) gives its confidence interval, where new_df is a DataFrame with the same column names as in the formula.
  • model.get_influence() gives the standardised residuals in .resid_studentized_internal and the Cook's distances in .cooks_distance[0].
  • model.rsquared and model.rsquared_adj give $R^2$ and the adjusted $R^2$.
  • In a formula, C(var) treats var as a categorical variable, and I(x**2) adds the higher-order term $x^2$.

The hypotheses are

$$ H_0: \beta_1 = 0 \quad \text{vs} \quad H_1: \beta_1 \ne 0 $$

where $\beta_1$ is the coefficient of $x$ in the simple model $y \sim x$.

Since this is a simple model, we can use either the $t$-test or the $F$-test:

  • $t$-test: the test statistic is $t = 11.622$, whose null distribution is $t_{98}$ (since $df = n - 2 = 98$). The $p$-value is less than 0.001.
  • $F$-test: the test statistic is $F = 135.1$, whose null distribution is $F_{1, 98}$. The $p$-value is the same as for the $t$-test. Note that $F = t^2 = 11.622^2 \approx 135.1$.

With a very small $p$-value, the data provide very strong evidence that Model 1 is significant, i.e. size is a significant regressor for the selling price. Hence, $y$ is not independent of $x$ and there is a significant linear relationship between them.

(g) Derive the $R^2$ of Model 1 and interpret it. Verify that $\sqrt{R^2} = |cor(y, x)|$. In which situation do we have $\sqrt{R^2} = cor(y, x)$?

In [12]:

R^2 = 0.5795

For Model 1, $R^2 = 0.5795$. This means that 57.95% of the variation in the selling price is explained by Model 1.

Indeed, $\sqrt{0.5795} = 0.7613 = |cor(y, x)|$.

In a simple model $y \sim x$, $\sqrt{R^2} = cor(y, x)$ when the estimated slope is positive (equivalently, $cor(y, x) > 0$). If the estimated slope is negative, $cor(y, x) = -\sqrt{R^2}$.

Question 4¶

A real estate agent in another town models the selling price $Y$ (dollars) of a house using its size $X$ (square feet) and the quadrant of the town where it is located. The variable Quadrant has four categories: NE, NW, SE and SW. The agent fits the model

$$ Y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X + \beta_2 I(\text{Quadrant}=\text{NE}) + \beta_3 I(\text{Quadrant}=\text{NW}) + \beta_4 I(\text{Quadrant}=\text{SE}) + \varepsilon, $$

where, for example, $I(\text{Quadrant}=\text{NE}) = 1$ if the house is in the NE quadrant and 0 otherwise.

(a) Quadrant has four categories. Why does the model contain only three indicator variables? Which quadrant is the baseline category?

A categorical variable with $k$ categories usually needs $k - 1$ indicator variables. Quadrant has 4 categories, so the model has 3 indicator variables.

The category without its own indicator variable is the baseline. Here, the baseline category is SW. So, for a house in SW, all three indicators equal 0.

(b) The fitted model is

$$ \hat{Y} = 10000 + 80X + 25000 I(\text{Quadrant}=\text{NE}) + 30000 I(\text{Quadrant}=\text{NW}) - 5000 I(\text{Quadrant}=\text{SE}). $$

Write down the fitted equations for houses in each of the four quadrants. Hence, estimate the mean selling price of houses of size 1500 square feet in the NE quadrant.

  • NE: $\hat{Y} = 10000 + 80X + 25000 = 35000 + 80X$
  • NW: $\hat{Y} = 10000 + 80X + 30000 = 40000 + 80X$
  • SE: $\hat{Y} = 10000 + 80X - 5000 = 5000 + 80X$
  • SW (baseline): $\hat{Y} = 10000 + 80X$

For a house of size 1500 square feet in NE:

$$ \hat{Y} = 35000 + 80(1500) = 155{,}000 \text{ dollars}. $$

(c) Interpret the coefficient 30000 of $I(\text{Quadrant}=\text{NW})$. For two houses of the same size, how much higher is the estimated mean selling price in NW than in NE?

For houses of the same size, the mean selling price in the NW quadrant is estimated to be 30,000 dollars higher than in the SW quadrant (the baseline).

Each indicator coefficient compares a quadrant with the baseline. To compare NW with NE, we take the difference of their coefficients: $30000 - 25000 = 5000$. For two houses of the same size, the estimated mean selling price in NW is 5,000 dollars higher than in NE.

(d) After fitting the model, the agent plots the standardised residuals against the fitted response and sees a clear funnel shape. Which model assumption is violated? Suggest a possible fix, and explain how the fix changes the interpretation of the new $\beta_1$.

A funnel shape means that the spread of the residuals changes with the fitted response, so the constant variance assumption is violated.

A possible fix is to transform the response, for example by fitting $\log(Y)$, $\sqrt{Y}$ or $1/Y$ instead of $Y$.

Transforming $Y$ changes the interpretation of the coefficients. For example, in the model

$$ \log(Y) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X + \beta_2 I(\text{Quadrant}=\text{NE}) + \beta_3 I(\text{Quadrant}=\text{NW}) + \beta_4 I(\text{Quadrant}=\text{SE}) + \varepsilon, $$

$\beta_1$ is the increase in $\log(Y)$, not in $Y$, for every one-unit increase in $X$, holding the quadrant fixed.
