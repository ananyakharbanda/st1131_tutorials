Residence Public Transport Walk / Cycle Private Transport Total
On campus 20 55 5 80
Off campus 75 15 30 120

(a) Calculate the percentage distribution of preferred mode of transport separately for students living on campus and off campus.

For students living on campus:

  • Public Transport: $\frac{20}{80}\times100 = 25.0\%$
  • Walk / Cycle: $\frac{55}{80}\times100 = 68.75\%$
  • Private Transport: $\frac{5}{80}\times100 = 6.25\%$

For students living off campus:

  • Public Transport: $\frac{75}{120}\times100 = 62.5\%$
  • Walk / Cycle: $\frac{15}{120}\times100 = 12.5\%$
  • Private Transport: $\frac{30}{120}\times100 = 25.0\%$

The percentages should be calculated separately within each residence group. Therefore, we divide by 80 for students living on campus and by 120 for students living off campus.

# Method 1: Using a proportion table with pandas .plot()
# Build a DataFrame from the raw counts, with transport modes as the row index 
# and residence type as columns.
modes = ["Public Transport", "Walk / Cycle", "Private Transport"]
df = pd.DataFrame({"On Campus": on_campus, "Off Campus": off_campus}, index=modes)
# Convert raw counts into percentages within each residence group.
df_proptab = df.div(df.sum(axis=0), axis=1) * 100
# Plot directly from the DataFrame — pandas automatically creates a grouped bar plot, 
# positioning "On Campus" and "Off Campus" bars side by side for each mode, 
# using the index as x-axis labels.
df_proptab.plot(kind="bar",edgecolor="black")
plt.xlabel("Preferred Mode of Transport")
plt.ylabel("Percentage of Students (%)")
plt.title("Preferred Mode of Transport by Place of Residence")
plt.legend(title="Residence")
plt.xticks(rotation=0)
plt.show()

# Method 2: Using plt.bar() with manual bar positioning
# Create numeric base positions for each mode (0, 1, 2, ...), since
# plt.bar() needs numeric x-coordinates to offset bars — it can't
# automatically group by a text label the way pandas .plot() does.
x = np.arange(len(modes))
width = 0.35
# Plot each residence group as its own set of bars, shifted left/right
# by half the bar width so the two bars for each mode sit side by side
# without overlapping.
plt.bar(x - width / 2, on_campus_pct, width, label="On Campus",edgecolor="black")
plt.bar(x + width / 2, off_campus_pct, width, label="Off Campus",edgecolor="black")
plt.xlabel("Preferred Mode of Transport")
plt.ylabel("Percentage of Students (%)")
plt.title("Preferred Mode of Transport by Place of Residence")
# Replace the numeric x-axis positions (0, 1, 2) with the actual mode names
plt.xticks(x, modes)

(c) Based on your calculations and graphical display, does there appear to be an association between place of residence and preferred mode of transport? Briefly explain.

Yes, there appears to be an association between place of residence and preferred mode of transport.

Students living on campus are much more likely to walk or cycle (68.75%), whereas students living off campus are much more likely to use public transport (62.5%). Private transport is also more common among students living off campus (25.0%) than among those living on campus (6.25%).

Since the distributions of preferred mode of transport differ noticeably between the two residence groups, the two variables appear to be associated.

(d) Calculate the difference in proportions and the relative risk of preferring public transport, comparing on-campus and off-campus students. What do these values suggest about the strength of the association?

For students living on campus,

$$ \hat{p}_{\text{on}} = \frac{20}{80} = 0.25 $$

For students living off campus,

$$ \hat{p}_{\text{off}} = \frac{75}{120} = 0.625 $$

The difference in proportions is

$$ \hat{p}_{\text{off}} - \hat{p}_{\text{on}} = 0.625 - 0.25 = 0.375 $$

Thus, the proportion of students who prefer public transport is 37.5 percentage points higher among students living off campus.

The relative risk is

$$ \frac{\hat{p}_{\text{off}}}{\hat{p}_{\text{on}}} = \frac{0.625}{0.25} = 2.5 $$

Thus, students living off campus are 2.5 times as likely to prefer public transport as students living on campus.

Both measures indicate a substantial difference between the two groups, suggesting an association between residence and preference for public transport.

The difference in proportions measures the absolute difference between the two groups, while the relative risk measures the difference on a relative scale.

A relative risk of 1 would indicate equal proportions in the two groups. Here, the relative risk is 2.5, which indicates a substantial difference in the likelihood of preferring public transport between off-campus and on-campus students.

(d) Another dataset has a correlation coefficient close to 0, but its scatter plot is shown in Figure 3. Does a correlation coefficient close to 0 necessarily imply that there is no association between two variables? Explain using Figure 3.


Figure 3

No. A correlation coefficient close to 0 does not necessarily imply that there is no association between two variables.

Figure 3 shows a clear non-linear association between $X$ and $Y$. As $X$ increases from negative values towards 0, $Y$ decreases; as $X$ increases beyond 0, $Y$ increases.

Therefore, although the correlation coefficient is close to 0, there is still a strong relationship between the two variables. The correlation coefficient is close to 0 because it measures the strength and direction of a linear relationship, and the relationship shown here is non-linear.

The correlation coefficient measures the strength and direction of a linear relationship. It does not measure all possible forms of association between two quantitative variables.

Therefore, a correlation coefficient close to 0 indicates little or no linear association, but a strong non-linear association may still exist. This is why a scatter plot should always be examined together with the correlation coefficient.
