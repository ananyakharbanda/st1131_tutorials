Everything works!

Some important functions we will use are:

  • stats.levene(x, y) performs the equal variance test, with $H_0$: the two populations have the same variance, against $H_1$: they have different variances.
  • stats.ttest_ind(x, y, equal_var, alternative) performs a two-sample $t$-test for independent samples, testing $\mu_X - \mu_Y$. Use equal_var=True if the equal variance test has a large $p$-value, and equal_var=False otherwise.
  • stats.ttest_rel(x, y, alternative) performs a paired (dependent samples) $t$-test. It is the same as a one-sample $t$-test on the differences x - y.
  • result.confidence_interval(0.95) gives the confidence interval from the result of a $t$-test, with its two ends stored in .low and .high. For ttest_ind, it is a confidence interval for $\mu_X - \mu_Y$.
  • stats.probplot(data, plot=plt) draws a QQ plot to check whether the data are approximately normal.
  • stats.shapiro(data) performs the Shapiro-Wilk test, with $H_0$: the sample is from a normal distribution, against $H_1$: it is not.

The alternative argument is "two-sided" (the default), "greater" or "less", and it always refers to the first sample minus the second.

Question 1¶

The $p$-value for testing $H_0: \mu = 100$ vs $H_1: \mu \ne 100$ is $0.043$, where $\mu$ is the mean of a population.

(a) What decision is made at significance level 0.05?

Since the $p$-value of 0.043 is less than 0.05, we reject $H_0$. At significance level $\alpha=0.05$, we conclude that the population mean $\mu$ is not equal to 100.

(b) If the decision in part (a) is wrong, what type of error has been made?

In part (a), we rejected $H_0$. If this decision is wrong, then $H_0$ is actually true, so this is a Type I error (rejecting $H_0$ when it is true).

This would happen if the true mean is in fact 100, but by chance we obtained a sample that gave a small $p$-value of 0.043, which led us to reject $H_0: \mu = 100$.

(c) Does a 95% confidence interval for $\mu$ contain 100? Explain.

No. A two-sided test at significance level $\alpha$ rejects $H_0: \mu = \mu_0$ exactly when $\mu_0$ lies outside the $100(1-\alpha)\%$ confidence interval for $\mu$.

Since the two-sided test rejects $H_0: \mu = 100$ at significance level 0.05, the 95% confidence interval for $\mu$ does not contain 100.

Assumptions:

  • The data were obtained by randomisation.
  • The variable (hours spent on the internet per week) is quantitative, and it is the same variable for both groups.
  • The two samples (females and males) are independent.
  • The histograms of both groups are very right-skewed, so neither population is normal. However, the sample sizes (778 females and 621 males) are very large, so the $t$-test can still be used.
  • From part (b), the two populations can be assumed to have equal variances, so we use equal_var=True.

Test statistic: $T = -1.050$, whose null distribution is $t_{1397}$ (since $df = 778 + 621 - 2 = 1397$).

$p$-value and conclusion: The $p$-value is 0.2940, which is greater than 0.01. We do not reject $H_0$. At significance level 0.01, there is no evidence that the mean number of hours spent on the internet per week differs between females and males.

t = -1.9166, df = 7, p-value = 0.0484

Since both measurements come from the same person, the two samples are dependent (matched pairs). We therefore analyse the differences $D = \text{Glaucoma} - \text{Unaffected}$.

Assumptions:

  • The data were obtained by randomisation.
  • The variable (corneal thickness) is quantitative.
  • With only 8 differences, the sample size is small, so the population of differences should be approximately normal. A histogram of 8 values tells us very little, so we use a QQ plot and the Shapiro-Wilk test. The points in the QQ plot lie reasonably close to the line, and the Shapiro-Wilk test has a $p$-value of 0.1276, which is greater than 0.05. We do not reject normality, so the normality assumption is reasonable.

Hypotheses: $$ H_0: \mu_D = 0 \quad \text{vs} \quad H_1: \mu_D < 0 $$ where $\mu_D$ is the population mean of the differences in corneal thickness (glaucoma eye minus unaffected eye).

Test statistic: $T = -1.917$, whose null distribution is $t_7$.

$p$-value and conclusion: The $p$-value is 0.0484, which is less than 0.05. We reject $H_0$. At significance level 0.05, there is evidence that glaucoma decreases the thickness of the cornea.

Note: stats.ttest_rel(glaucoma, unaffected) gives exactly the same result as stats.ttest_1samp(diff, popmean=0), since a paired $t$-test is a one-sample $t$-test on the differences.

(b) Suppose that we ignore the fact that the data are paired. What would the conclusion be at the same significance level? (You may skip the normality check for this part.)

If we ignore the pairing, we would treat the two samples as independent and use a two-sample $t$-test.

The equal variance test has a large $p$-value of 0.8292, so we assume equal variances and use equal_var=True.

Hypotheses: $$ H_0: \mu_X - \mu_Y = 0 \quad \text{vs} \quad H_1: \mu_X - \mu_Y < 0 $$ where $\mu_X$ and $\mu_Y$ are the population mean corneal thicknesses of eyes with glaucoma and unaffected eyes respectively.

Test statistic: $T = -0.416$, whose null distribution is $t_{14}$ (since $df = 8 + 8 - 2 = 14$).

$p$-value and conclusion: The $p$-value is 0.3417, which is greater than 0.05. We do not reject $H_0$, and there is no evidence that glaucoma decreases the thickness of the cornea.

Note the different conclusions at the same significance level: in part (a) we reject $H_0$, while in part (b) we do not. Corneal thickness varies a lot from person to person (sample standard deviations of about 28 to 31), and the independent samples test treats all of this as noise. The paired test removes this person-to-person variation by looking at the differences within each person, whose standard deviation is only about 9.2. Analysing the data in the wrong way can therefore lead to the wrong conclusion.
